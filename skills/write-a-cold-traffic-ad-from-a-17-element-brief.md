# Write a cold-traffic ad from a 17-element brief

Derick Carneiro's method for direct-response video ads aimed at people who don't know the brand. The ad has to make four "sales" before the product is pitched: the content, hope, value and impulse sales. Each sale is built from "reasons why" the viewer already wants to hear, and there are 17 of them in total. You find those reasons from facts about the product and from ads that are already winning, fill them into a brief, then write the brief into the structure of a proven reference ad.

## The 17 elements (his ad psychology)

Stage 1, the content sale (make them want to watch to the end):
1. Format: why the viewer relaxes and doesn't suspect a sales pitch.
2. Topic: why the viewer might care about what the ad says.
3. Concept: why they feel they have to watch to the end.
4. Visual hook: why they snap out of autopilot and stop scrolling.
5. Copy hook: why they decide to keep watching.

Stage 2, the hope sale (why it failed before and why this works):
6. Identification: why they feel understood.
7. Point of contact: what they already suspect causes the problem.
8. Unique mechanism of the problem: the missing reason that proves their suspicion right.
9. Unique mechanism of the function: the action needed to fix it, and why other solutions can't do it.
10. Unique mechanism of the solution: the one thing that performs that action, with proof.
11. Conditions: why other solutions don't offer it, and what makes it rare or hard to get.

Stage 3, the value sale (why this product):
12. Unique selling proposition: why this product is the best or only place to get the mechanism under the right conditions.
13. Features: only the components that raise the odds of the result, make the dream outcome tangible, cut effort or shorten the timeline.
14. Anchoring: why not solving the problem costs more (money, emotion, time) than the price.

Stage 4, the impulse sale (why now):
15. Risk reversal: why not buying is riskier than buying.
16. What they gain by buying now.
17. What they lose by waiting.

## Steps

1. Premises. List the facts you already have from the product page: brand, product, ingredients or components, attributes, price and bundles, guarantee, what buyers gain by buying now, and what they lose by waiting. Use only real facts.
2. Pick the market (a group sharing one desire). For a running brand, use the market its highest-traffic landing pages and most-launched ads of the last 60 days target. For an untested product, list the markets it could serve, drop any with fewer than three competitors, and pick the one whose competitors grew traffic most in the last 30 days. He uses the GetHookd ad library (with its MCP and Claude) and TrendTrack for this. Don't gamble on a new market: save the risk for concept, format and hook.
3. Pick the segment: a cluster within the market that shares a condition, identity or behaviour. Either reuse the segment that winning ads (yours or competitors') already target, or read Reddit threads to find one nobody is targeting.
4. Profile the avatar in two layers.
   - Psychographics: primary desire, main problem, secondary desires, pain points, dreams, fears. Take these from winning ads, funnels and past sales letters in the same market rather than doing days of fresh research. They barely change over time.
   - Identity: demographics; behaviour (which competitor ads, videos, trends, hobbies, news and shows catch their attention, and any related conditions); perspective (beliefs, biases, enemies, allies, language). Put most of your research effort into behaviour, because that's where topics, formats, concepts and hooks come from.
5. Brief the mechanism by answering four questions. Which point of contact does this avatar want to hear? Which problem mechanism are they most ready to accept? Which function mechanism? Which solution mechanism? Connect dots they already believe rather than teaching something new.
6. Brief the content.
   - Topic: list candidate facts, topics or situations the avatar cares about, using search demand and what winning ads cover, then pick one.
   - Format: the look, narrative style and characters, with their roles. Reuse elements from ads already performing, and place at most one deliberate new bet.
   - Concept: combine characters, narrative and topic into a one-line logline, then find the angle that makes the avatar need to hear more.
   - Copy hook: pick a hook structure that's proven in ad libraries and fits the concept, then write it.
   - Visual hook: something that fits the format and concept but breaks expectations.
7. Brief the offer.
   - Conditions: answer "why can't I just get this myself?"
   - Unique selling proposition: show that the product meets each of those conditions, one by one, using only facts from the premises.
   - Features: map each one to odds, dream, effort or time, and drop any that map to none.
   - Anchoring: compare the daily price with the money, emotional and time cost of not solving the problem.
8. Brief the close. Risk reversal (for example, a guarantee longer than the time results take), the gain from buying now (bundles, shipping), and the loss from waiting. Use only real deadlines and stock limits.
9. Write. Download and transcribe a proven reference ad in the same market. Split it into mini-blocks by job (hook, belief break, mechanism reveal, product presentation and so on), then fill each block from the brief. You can do this by hand, or give a writing model the brief and the reference's block structure. The brief decides what gets said. The reference only decides the order and the rhythm.
10. Batch by risk. Each new bet (concept, format, hook, segment) raises an ad's risk. For a scaling brand, run a few conservative ads, many moderate ones and some aggressive ones.
11. Success check (added, not in source): every one of the 17 elements is filled in the brief with a reason, each factual claim traces back to a premise, and the script follows the reference's block order.

## Anti-patterns

- Opening a cold-traffic ad with price, product or offer.
- Letting AI write the ad without a brief. He says this is why most AI-written ads fail.
- Listing ingredients or features that don't serve odds, dream, effort or time.
- Spending days on psychographic research that winning ads already contain, while neglecting behaviour research.
- Betting on a new market when the gain is in a new concept, format or hook.
- Promising fast results the avatar has stopped believing.
- (Added, not in source.) Making health, medical, income or financial claims that the product page and the law don't support. His worked example is a capsaicin supplement for men with diabetes and erectile dysfunction, using a sexualised video-game character and a Black Friday "stock running out" close. Platform ad policies and consumer law in Australia and elsewhere restrict health claims, sexual content and false scarcity. For Niche Connect or any financial-adviser client, financial-promotion rules apply too. Check every claim before an ad runs.
