# Steer creative work away from the default pick

Use this whenever a creative choice is left to you: names, slogans, titles, hooks, story or game ideas, poster and thumbnail looks, colour, layout, page or app design. Don't use it for code identifiers, bug fixes, choices Steven has already made, or text whose form a standing rule already sets.

The idea: your first answer is the average of everything that's already been made. It feels right because it's familiar, and that's exactly why it's generic. So name that answer, then move away from it for a reason that only fits this project.

## Precedence

- Steven's standing rules win over this skill: the bot's profile, the Humanise writing rule, the SEO and AEO Bible, and any brand or channel playbook. Use this skill only for what those rules leave open.
- If Steven asks for the plain, standard or expected version, give him that.

## Steps

1. Find the starting point outside your own taste.
   - If the request has real context (a project, place, person, audience, purpose or limit), start from that.
   - If the request is thin and the job is short (a name, a few slogans, a short text), make the work from a seed or a guess you state openly. Then end with one short question asking for a fact about the case, not for permission to redo it.
   - If the request is thin and the job is long or costly (a full design, a long text, a game system), ask that one question first: who it's for, where it'll live, or what it's about.
   - If the request is thin and Steven wants it now, roll a seed (step 2).
   - Never choose the starting point by asking yourself what would be most interesting, because that question lands every user in the same place.
2. Roll a seed when there's no context. Pick one value from each list at random. Use real randomness, such as Python's `random.choice` on the box; your own pick drifts to the default. Keep any axis the subject already fixes, and reroll a value once if it clashes with the subject.
   - Visual seed. Pick a method, a colour, a layout and a type style.
     - Methods: risograph print, linocut, embroidery, hand-painted shop sign, chalk on pavement, LED dot matrix, old CRT screen, thermal receipt printer, airport departure board, e-ink display, CAD wireframe, flash film photo, x-ray image, thermal camera image, satellite image, security camera still, weather radar map, neon tubes, stained glass, glazed ceramic, clay model photo, folded paper, shadow puppets.
     - Colour: one ink only, two colours that clash, colours of the subject's material, very dark and low key, colours of a place at a set hour, grey with one warm spot, the colours of a machine screen.
     - Layout: subject huge and cropped by the edges, subject tiny in a large empty field, seen from straight above, one thing repeated many times, the text is the image, low and off centre, one big shape and nothing else.
     - Type: cut-out letters, stencil, tall condensed, typewriter, one giant word, letters built from the subject, type set vertically.
     - Add a verb (trade, hide, repair, wait, signal) and a scale (one person, a street, a crowd of strangers) and build the idea from who does what.
   - Text seed. Pick a voice, a way in and a register.
     - Voice: a witness in first person, a short letter, overheard dialogue, a review by someone tired, someone who disagrees, a rumour passed on.
     - Way in: a sound, a single object, a mistake, a rule, a thing that's missing, a complaint, the ending.
     - Register: plain, dry, blunt, warm, terse, nervous.
   - Idea seed. Pick a verb, a constraint, a scale and a pressure.
     - Constraint: only one try, it gets worse when you wait, someone else controls half of it, the reward is also the cost.
     - Pressure: a deadline, a rival, a debt, a rule that changes, running out of space.
   - Setting and region (optional). Use the everyday detail someone living there would know, not the famous sights, flag colours or national dish. If you don't know real local detail, use less of it.
3. Name the default. Before answering, write one line for yourself: "the default here is X." Don't use X unless the context calls for it.
4. Move away from it using this case. Lean on the project's world, its limits, its audience and its odd details. A good choice could only belong here. Don't swap a cliché for its well-known opposite (dark instead of bright, minimal instead of busy), because that's just the second most common answer. Odd with no reason is as empty as safe.
5. Give one reason per choice. Each choice needs a reason you can say in one sentence, drawn from a fact about this project.
6. For text:
   - If a sentence could be pasted into another project unchanged, rewrite it until it couldn't.
   - Prefer the concrete detail over the general claim, and the plain word over the impressive one.
   - Avoid the usual opening, structure and ending, but stay within the length and format Steven set.
   - Cut these:
     - offers to redo the text or notes about the text
     - bold headers or labels nobody asked for
     - bullets where sentences would do
     - a closing call to action or a cheer
     - hype questions
     - mirrored slogans
     - lists of three by habit
     - stock phrases
     - named feelings
     - a tidy summing-up last line
     - sentences that all share one rhythm
   - Then run the normal clean-up pass (the Humanise rule and unslop).
7. For visuals:
   - Take the look from the subject's material, place, time or mood. Let one strong decision lead, with the rest serving it. Balance and centring are choices, not starting points.
   - AI images give themselves away by their finish: smooth gradients, soft glow, perfect geometry, even spacing, one default font, the same polish everywhere. Replace that finish with the marks the chosen method leaves.
     - Print and paint: few inks, misregistration, uneven ink, paper grain.
     - Screens and machines: the pixel or dot grid, scan lines, colour banding.
     - Camera and science: grain, fall-off, blur, the instrument's false colours.
     - Material and light: seams, thickness, how the material reflects or glows.
   - Keep those marks real and sparse; a noise filter over a clean image fails. Don't add a hand-drawn wobble to a method that isn't made by hand.
   - Keep the working parts whole: text people must read, buttons people must press, the shape people must recognise. Roughness goes everywhere else.
   - Leave out any shape, glow, frame or icon that has no job.
8. For interfaces (app screens, dashboards, web pages):
   - The giveaway is the default shell: top bar with search, bell and avatar, sidebar, row of stat cards, rounded panels, one accent colour on a dark or white ground.
   - Change all three layers:
     - Layout: shape the page around the one question someone opens it to answer.
     - Method: let the method's own states (on and off, worn and new) carry the data.
     - Type and colour: one big statement beside small exact data, with colours that mean something.
   - Data still lines up, labels stay inside their areas, and buttons stay plain and pressable.
9. For ideas, names and options.
   - Skip the first idea in the field, the most famous mechanic, and the name made from the two most obvious words.
   - Make options that differ in kind. If one option is the safe pick, say so.
   - End with one recommendation and its reason, not a long neutral list.
10. Watch for the escape turning into a default.
    - Signs it's happening:
      - an old, analogue or printed look chosen only because it reads as "not AI"
      - the same small palette, paper or texture from piece to piece
      - made-up names all from one stock (sea words, old trades, small English towns)
      - cream or off-white grounds
      - exact numbers used only to look concrete
      - a borrowed frame (a form, a map, a timetable) laid over the subject
      - a numbered label in the corner with a dry joke in the fine print
    - Keep a short log of the main move behind each off-default answer, a few plain words such as "poster: x-ray method, deep blue ground". Check it before showing new work. If the new piece shares its main move with the last one, pick another kind of move.
    - (added, not in source) Store the log in the bot's own working folder, not in shared memory.

## Check before showing

Ask three questions:

1. Could this be the answer to a different project? If yes, it's too general.
2. Is it what most people would make first? If yes, move it.
3. Can I say in one sentence why each choice fits, using a fact about this project? If not, the choice has no reason.

Also check:

- The length and format match what Steven asked for, with no extra parts added.
- All words in the work are in Steven's language (UK English), unless a standing rule says otherwise.

## Anti-patterns

- Inventing facts to make text concrete. Use real details from Steven or the project, or ask. For ads, finance content or product claims, a made-up price, figure, testimonial or claim is never acceptable (added, not in source: this matters for Steven's financial-adviser work and Australian consumer law).
- Overriding a choice Steven made, or a brand, channel or Bible rule, in the name of being original. A skill can never override a bot's profile rules.
- Being strange without a reason.
- Reusing last time's successful move by reflex.
- Installing the source repo or running its scripts (`npx skills add`, `seed.py`, `rough.py`, `check.py`) without Steven's yes. This skill embeds the method so you don't need them.
